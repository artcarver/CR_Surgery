00

Decode the alphabet soup

Glossary — read this once
▾

Ward & presentation shorthand

  • POD# — postoperative day number (POD0 = day of surgery). First thing you state for any post-op patient.
  • Pre-rounding — seeing/charting your patients alone before team rounds. One-liner — the summary sentence: age, history, POD#, operation, why they're here.
  • SOAP note — Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan. Pimping — rapid-fire questioning to test/teach. Scut — the non-glamorous task work; doing it well earns goodwill.
  • NPO — nothing by mouth. I&O (I/Os) — intake and output (fluids in vs urine/drain/stoma out); a core daily number. DRE — digital rectal exam.
  • CBC / BMP / CMP — complete blood count / basic / comprehensive metabolic panel. WBC, Hgb, Cr, Mg, lactate — white cells, hemoglobin, creatinine, magnesium, lactate (perfusion/sepsis marker).

Anatomy & vessels

  • SMA / IMA — superior / inferior mesenteric artery. SMV / IMV — corresponding veins. SRA — superior rectal artery; TI — terminal ileum.
  • TME — total mesorectal excision (key rectal-cancer dissection). CME / CVL — complete mesocolic excision / central vascular ligation (colon-cancer equivalent).
  • CRM — circumferential (radial) resection margin; MRF — mesorectal fascia; EMVI — extramural vascular invasion (rectal-MRI/path terms).
  • IAS / EAS — internal / external anal sphincter. Dentate (pectinate) line — anatomic divide of the anal canal (epithelium, nerves, blood supply all change here).

Operations & procedures

  • LAR — low anterior resection (rectal cancer, sphincter-preserving). APR — abdominoperineal resection (removes anus → permanent colostomy).
  • TAC — total abdominal colectomy; IRA — ileorectal anastomosis. IPAA — ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (the "J-pouch"). Hartmann — resection + end colostomy + closed rectal stump.
  • TaTME — transanal TME; TAMIS / TEM — transanal minimally invasive / endoscopic microsurgery (local rectal excision). EMR / ESD — endoscopic mucosal resection / submucosal dissection.
  • LIFT — ligation of intersphincteric fistula tract; LIS — lateral internal sphincterotomy (fissures). I&D — incision and drainage. CRS / HIPEC — cytoreductive surgery / hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemo.
  • ICG — indocyanine green (fluorescent perfusion dye). Brooke ileostomy — standard spouted ileostomy; end vs loop — single-lumen vs diverting two-opening stoma.

Cancer staging & pathology

  • CRC / LARC / mCRC — colorectal cancer / locally advanced rectal cancer / metastatic CRC. TNM — Tumor/Node/Metastasis staging; AJCC — publishes it.
  • CEA — carcinoembryonic antigen (trend marker, not screening). pCR / cCR / MPR — pathologic / clinical complete response / major pathologic response.
  • LVI / PNI — lymphovascular / perineural invasion (high-risk features). IHC / NGS — immunohistochemistry / next-generation sequencing.

Molecular markers & genes

  • MMR — mismatch repair; dMMR / pMMR — deficient / proficient. MSI / MSI-H / MSS — microsatellite instability / high / stable (the genomic signature of dMMR).
  • ctDNA — circulating tumor DNA; MRD — minimal/molecular residual disease. KRAS / NRAS / BRAF / PIK3CA / HER2 — mutations/markers guiding targeted drugs.
  • APC, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM, MUTYH — genes behind hereditary CRC syndromes. EGFR — target of cetuximab/panitumumab. PD-1 / PD-L1 / CTLA-4 — immune checkpoints blocked by immunotherapy.
  • Lynch / HNPCC, FAP, MAP — hereditary nonpolyposis CRC / familial adenomatous polyposis / MUTYH-associated polyposis.

Chemo, drugs & immunotherapy

  • 5-FU — 5-fluorouracil (backbone); leucovorin — potentiates 5-FU; capecitabine — oral 5-FU prodrug.
  • FOLFOX / mFOLFOX6 — 5-FU + leucovorin + oxaliplatin. CAPOX / XELOX — capecitabine + oxaliplatin. FOLFIRI — 5-FU + leucovorin + irinotecan; FOLFIRINOX — adds both.
  • Oxaliplatin — platinum; signature toxicity is cumulative neuropathy. ICI — immune checkpoint inhibitor (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, dostarlimab, atezolizumab; ipilimumab = anti-CTLA-4).
  • Anti-EGFR — cetuximab, panitumumab (only in RAS wild-type, left-sided). Bevacizumab — anti-VEGF (anti-angiogenesis).

Treatment-strategy terms

  • Neoadjuvant — therapy before surgery; adjuvant — after. TNT — total neoadjuvant therapy (all chemo + radiation before surgery).
  • CRT / SCRT / LCRT — chemoradiation / short-course RT / long-course chemoradiation. W&W / NOM — watch-and-wait / non-operative management.
  • DFS / OS / PFS / RFS — disease-free / overall / progression-free / recurrence-free survival. NCCN — writes the cancer-treatment guidelines.

Perioperative, complications & management

  • ERAS — Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (the perioperative pathway you round inside). MBP — mechanical bowel prep; ORS — oral rehydration solution.
  • VTE / DVT / PE — venous thromboembolism / DVT / pulmonary embolism. LMWH — low-molecular-weight heparin; SCD — sequential compression device.
  • SSI — surgical site infection; AKI — acute kidney injury. SBO / LBO — small / large bowel obstruction; ileus — sluggish non-mechanical bowel.
  • NG tube — nasogastric (decompresses stomach); Foley — urinary catheter. TAP block — transversus abdominis plane block (regional analgesia).
  • MAP — mean arterial pressure (≥65 intraop); PONV — post-op nausea/vomiting; LOS — length of stay; ASA class — anesthesia risk grade. Alvimopan — speeds return of bowel function; loperamide — anti-diarrheal for high stoma output.

Disease & benign-condition terms

  • IBD / UC / CD — inflammatory bowel disease / ulcerative colitis / Crohn's; ASUC — acute severe UC. Anti-TNF — anti-tumor-necrosis-factor biologic (e.g., infliximab).
  • LARS — low anterior resection syndrome. Hinchey — severity grading for complicated diverticulitis (I–IV). Goodsall's rule — predicts the path of an anal fistula.
  • C. diff — Clostridioides difficile (post-antibiotic colitis bug). HPV — human papillomavirus (cause of anal squamous cancer).

Trials you'll hear name-dropped

  • PROSPECT — selected rectal cancers can skip radiation (FOLFOX + selective chemoradiation). OPRA / RAPIDO / PRODIGE 23 — established TNT and organ preservation in rectal cancer.
  • CheckMate 8HW / KEYNOTE-177 — immunotherapy first-line for metastatic dMMR CRC. ATOMIC — adjuvant atezolizumab + FOLFOX for stage III dMMR colon. NICHE-2 / NICHE-3 — neoadjuvant immunotherapy for dMMR colon.
  • BREAKWATER — first-line encorafenib + cetuximab + chemo for BRAF V600E mCRC. DYNAMIC / CIRCULATE / ORACLE — ctDNA-guided adjuvant trials (ORACLE enrolls at UCSD). IDEA — defined 3- vs 6-month adjuvant chemo duration.
01

Anatomy you will actually be asked about

Segments · vessels · watershed · planes · sphincters · nerves
▾

Segments, fixation & surface anatomy

  • Order: cecum → ascending → hepatic flexure → transverse → splenic flexure → descending → sigmoid → rectum (12–15 cm) → anal canal (~2–5 cm).
  • Fixation (dictates what gets mobilized): ascending and descending colon are retroperitoneal (fixed); transverse and sigmoid are intraperitoneal on a mesentery (mobile). Flexures must be taken down for anastomotic reach, and the mobile sigmoid & cecum are the segments that volvulize.
  • Taeniae coli: three longitudinal bands that converge at the appendiceal base (landmark for the appendix) and again at the rectosigmoid junction, fanning into the continuous longitudinal muscle of the rectum (rectum has no taeniae, haustra, or appendices epiploicae).

Arterial supply, by embryologic origin

  • Midgut → SMA: cecum, ascending, hepatic flexure, proximal ⅔ transverse. Branches: ileocolic (most consistent, supplies cecum/TI), right colic (variable, often absent), middle colic.
  • Hindgut → IMA: distal ⅓ transverse, descending, sigmoid, upper rectum. Branches: left colic, sigmoidal branches, terminating as the superior rectal (hemorrhoidal) artery.
  • Why oncologic resections look the way they do: take the whole vascular pedicle plus draining lymphatics. Right cancer → ligate ileocolic ± right/middle colic (right hemicolectomy); left → high IMA ligation. Margin is dictated by blood supply & lymphatics, not just tumor edge. Examine a minimum of 12 lymph nodes for accurate staging.

Collaterals & watershed zones (high-yield for ischemia)

  • Marginal artery of Drummond: continuous collateral arcade along the mesenteric border connecting SMA & IMA territories; perfuses colon after a pedicle is ligated.
  • Arc of Riolan (meandering mesenteric artery): a more central, variable SMA–IMA collateral; prominent with proximal SMA/IMA stenosis.
  • Griffiths point (splenic flexure): watershed between middle colic (SMA) & left colic (IMA) — #1 site of ischemic colitis. Sudeck point (rectosigmoid): watershed between last sigmoidal branch & superior rectal artery.
  • Clinical payoff: after high IMA ligation, distal perfusion hinges on the marginal artery via the SMA. A poor marginal artery = a tenuous, leak-prone anastomosis — one reason surgeons mobilize the splenic flexure for a well-perfused, tension-free reach.

Venous & lymphatic drainage (explains metastatic patterns)

  • Colon and upper rectum → portal system (SMV/IMV → portal vein) → liver is the first and most common met site.
  • Lower rectum/anal canal → partly systemic via middle/inferior rectal veins → internal iliac → IVC, so low lesions can metastasize to lung directly, bypassing the liver.
  • Lymphatics follow arteries. Above the dentate line → internal iliac/mesenteric nodes; below → inguinal nodes (why anal canal cancers get groin attention).

Rectum & the surgical planes

  • Rectum ~12–15 cm from the anal verge; three transverse folds (valves of Houston). No true mesentery — it carries the mesorectum, a fascial envelope of fat, vessels, lymphatics.
  • Total mesorectal excision (TME): sharp dissection in the avascular areolar "holy plane" between mesorectal fascia (visceral) and presacral/parietal fascia. Sharp TME (Heald) dramatically reduced local recurrence vs blunt technique.
  • Circumferential resection margin (CRM): the radial margin. Positive/threatened CRM (≤1 mm) is the strongest predictor of local recurrence and a key reason for neoadjuvant therapy. Pelvic MRI pre-op assesses tumor vs mesorectal fascia.
  • Rectal arterial supply: superior rectal (IMA), middle rectal (internal iliac), inferior rectal (internal pudendal from internal iliac).

  • You can band internal hemorrhoids in clinic (visceral, insensate) but never an external one (somatic, exquisitely painful).
  • Anal transition zone sits just above the dentate line — relevant in IPAA (a residual cuff can develop "cuffitis" or dysplasia).

Sphincter complex & continence

  • Internal anal sphincter (IAS): smooth muscle, involuntary, autonomic; continuation of the rectal circular muscle; provides most resting tone.
  • External anal sphincter (EAS): skeletal muscle, voluntary (pudendal n.); continuous superiorly with puborectalis and levator ani.
  • Puborectalis sling maintains the anorectal angle — key to gross continence. The palpable upper edge on DRE is the anorectal ring; dividing it causes incontinence (limits how aggressive a fistulotomy can be).

Pelvic autonomic nerves (the ones surgeons protect)

  • Superior hypogastric plexus / hypogastric nerves (sympathetic, L1–L3): ejaculation, bladder-neck closure. Injury → retrograde ejaculation, bladder dysfunction. At risk at the IMA origin and sacral promontory.
  • Pelvic splanchnic / inferior hypogastric (nervi erigentes) plexus (parasympathetic, S2–S4): erection, detrusor contraction. Injury → erectile and bladder dysfunction. At risk along the lateral pelvic sidewalls during low dissection.
  • "Nerve-sparing TME" is the deliberate preservation of these planes — a major quality-of-life issue, especially in younger patients.
02

The operations — decode the eponyms & know the why

Resections · pouches · technique concepts
▾

The core resections

  • Right hemicolectomy: cecum/ascending ± hepatic flexure; ileocolic anastomosis. Generally lower leak risk (small bowel to colon, excellent supply; stapled ileocolic leaks less than handsewn). Extended right adds the transverse for hepatic flexure/proximal transverse lesions.
  • Left hemicolectomy / sigmoidectomy: descending/sigmoid; colocolonic or colorectal anastomosis. Higher leak risk than right-sided.
  • Low anterior resection (LAR): rectal cancer with sphincter preservation. TME + colorectal or coloanal anastomosis. Low (<5 cm from verge), coloanal, and IPAA anastomoses are usually protected by a diverting loop ileostomy.
  • Abdominoperineal resection (APR): very low rectal/anal cancers where the sphincter can't be spared. Removes rectum and anus → permanent end colostomy. The perineal wound is large and slow-healing (sometimes flap-reconstructed) — watch it on rounds.
  • Hartmann procedure: segmental (usually sigmoid/rectal) resection with end colostomy + oversewn rectal stump, no anastomosis. Classic for perforated diverticulitis or obstructing left-sided cancer in a contaminated/unstable field. "Reversal" is a real, sometimes difficult second operation; a meaningful fraction are never reversed.
  • Total abdominal colectomy (TAC): entire colon removed; ileorectal anastomosis (elective) or end ileostomy (emergent/sick). Indications: UC, fulminant C. diff, synchronous tumors, attenuated FAP/Lynch, slow-transit constipation.
  • Total proctocolectomy: colon + rectum + anus (end ileostomy) or with IPAA (sphincter-preserving).

Restorative & specialized

  • Restorative proctocolectomy with IPAA ("J-pouch"): definitive operation for UC and FAP. Removes colon & rectum, builds an ileal reservoir anastomosed to the anus, restoring transanal defecation. Usually staged. Complications: pouchitis (most common long-term), leak, pelvic sepsis, cuffitis, pouch fistula, pouch failure.
  • TaTME (transanal TME): bottom-up dissection for difficult low rectal tumors, obese/narrow male pelvis. Note the local-recurrence controversy — Norway paused TaTME nationally over early recurrence signals; reserved for selected cases at experienced centers.
  • Local options: transanal excision / TAMIS / TEM for select early (T1, favorable) rectal lesions; polypectomy/EMR/ESD endoscopically for early lesions.

Technique concepts to recognize in the OR

  • Complete mesocolic excision (CME) / central vascular ligation (CVL): the colon-cancer analogue of TME — dissect embryologic planes and ligate the feeding vessel at its origin for a complete mesenteric envelope and maximal node yield.
  • Intracorporeal vs extracorporeal anastomosis: in MIS right colectomy, intracorporeal anastomosis is associated with faster bowel-function recovery and fewer wound/hernia issues.
  • ICG fluorescence angiography: IV indocyanine green to confirm anastomotic perfusion intraoperatively — a modifiable leak risk you can sometimes see before committing.
  • Referral-center reality: expect many redo / reversal / completion cases (Hartmann reversals, ileostomy takedowns, redo pouches, recurrent Crohn's, re-resections) → dense adhesions, longer cases, higher complication rates.

Identify it before rounds — it tells you the operation
▾

Ileostomy vs colostomy

  • End vs loop: End = single lumen, distal bowel removed/oversewn (APR, Hartmann, end ileostomy after TAC). Loop = two openings on one stoma, almost always temporary/diverting to protect a downstream anastomosis (loop ileostomy after LAR or IPAA).
  • Diverting loop ileostomy is preferred over loop transverse colostomy for protecting low anastomoses — lower prolapse, parastomal hernia, and reversal complications.

High ostomy output — the thing you'll actually manage

  • A new ileostomy putting out >1200–1500 mL/day drives dehydration, AKI, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic acidosis. Dehydration from high ostomy output is the leading cause of readmission after colorectal surgery.
  • Management ladder: anti-motility agents (loperamide, often high-dose; add diphenoxylate-atropine) → bulking/thickening (fiber, banana flakes) → oral rehydration solution rather than free water (hypotonic fluid worsens sodium loss) → PPI/H2 blocker to reduce secretory volume → hold diuretics/laxatives → aggressively replete magnesium → somatostatin analogs if refractory.
  • Round on the number: "Ileostomy put out 1,400 over 24h, making good urine, lytes stable, repleted Mg" signals competence.

Viability & complications

  • Viability: pink/red = healthy; dusky/black = ischemia → escalate now. A small rim of necrosis above fascia may be watched; necrosis below fascia is an emergency.
  • A stoma that stops + distension + nausea = obstruction or ileus, not "it's resting." Loop ileostomy "outlet obstruction" is a known early complication.
  • Chronic: parastomal hernia, prolapse, retraction, stenosis, high-output dermatitis.

Colorectal oncology

The part that's changing fastest — re-verify against NCCN
▾

Workup & staging

  • Colon: full colonoscopy with biopsy + tattoo of the lesion, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, baseline CEA. Clear the whole colon (synchronous cancers ~3–5%).
  • Rectal adds: pelvic MRI (T stage, nodes, CRM, EMVI) and often endorectal ultrasound for early lesions. Local staging drives neoadjuvant decisions.

  • CEA is surveillance/trend, not screening. A rising CEA after curative resection = recurrence until proven otherwise.
  • Screening & epidemiology: early-onset CRC (<50) is rising; average-risk screening now starts at age 45 (ACS/USPSTF). Options: colonoscopy, FIT, stool-DNA, and the blood-based cfDNA test (Shield) — FDA-approved (2024), NCCN-recognized (2025) for average-risk 45+; colonoscopy remains gold standard and is required for any positive non-colonoscopy test.
  • Stage IV / advanced: colorectal liver metastases can be resected for cure when feasible, sometimes after conversion chemotherapy downsizes them. Peritoneal mets: CRS ± HIPEC at specialized centers — though PRODIGE 7 questioned the added benefit of HIPEC (mitomycin) over complete cytoreduction alone.

Molecular testing every tumor gets — and why

  • Universal MMR/MSI testing on all CRC (IHC for MLH1/MSH2/MSH6/PMS2, or PCR/NGS for MSI). Two reasons:
  • 1. Lynch screening. Loss of staining flags possible Lynch → germline testing + genetics. If MLH1 is lost, reflex BRAF V600E and/or MLH1 promoter methylation — if positive, likely sporadic (methylation-driven), not Lynch.
  • 2. Therapy & prognosis. dMMR/MSI-high tumors respond to ICIs and resist single-agent fluoropyrimidine. In stage II, dMMR is a good prognostic sign and argues against adjuvant 5-FU monotherapy.
  • RAS/RAF & targeted therapy (metastatic): KRAS/NRAS mutations → no benefit from anti-EGFR (cetuximab/panitumumab); those work only in RAS wild-type. Even then, right-sided primaries respond poorly to anti-EGFR — sidedness guides first-line biologic (anti-EGFR for left, bevacizumab for right).
  • BRAF V600E (~10%, poor prognosis): BREAKWATER established first-line encorafenib + cetuximab + mFOLFOX6, roughly doubling median OS (~30 months) — FDA fully approved (2026). Now first-line, not just salvage (older BEACON used it after progression).
  • KRAS G12C: sotorasib or adagrasib + cetuximab (CodeBreaK 300), later lines. HER2 amplification: tucatinib + trastuzumab (MOUNTAINEER), trastuzumab-deruxtecan — actionable in RAS/BRAF wild-type.

Adjuvant chemotherapy after colon resection

  • Stage III (node-positive): adjuvant chemo is standard — FOLFOX or CAPOX/XELOX, ~15% absolute survival benefit.
  • Duration (IDEA): 3 vs 6 months risk-adapted. Lower-risk (T1–3, N1) often 3 months of CAPOX (less oxaliplatin neurotoxicity); higher-risk (T4 or N2) generally 6 months.
  • Oxaliplatin's signature toxicity: cumulative, dose-dependent peripheral sensory neuropathy (and cold dysesthesia) — the reason shorter courses are attractive.
  • Stage II: chemo is selective, for high-risk features — T4, <12 nodes, poor differentiation, LVI/PNI, obstruction or perforation. dMMR stage II generally should NOT get 5-FU monotherapy.

Immunotherapy across dMMR/MSI-high (~10–15% of CRC) — moving earlier

  • Metastatic: nivolumab + ipilimumab is now first-line (CheckMate 8HW) — 2-yr PFS ~72% vs ~14% with chemo; pembrolizumab monotherapy (KEYNOTE-177) is the single-agent option. Chemo is comparatively ineffective here.
  • Stage III colon — adjuvant (ATOMIC, ASCO 2025 plenary): adding atezolizumab to FOLFOX improved 3-yr DFS to ~86% vs ~77% (HR ~0.50) — practice-changing for dMMR stage III.
  • Locally advanced colon — neoadjuvant (NICHE-2/NICHE-3): short course of nivolumab + ipilimumab (or nivo + relatlimab) before surgery → ~98–99% pathologic response, ~68% pCR; NICHE-2 reported ~100% 3-yr DFS. Open question: neoadjuvant immuno may beat adjuvant and allow de-escalation of chemo & surgery.
  • Bottom line: if a dMMR patient is going to medical oncology before (or instead of) the OR, immunotherapy is usually why — and the field is pushing it earlier.

Rectal cancer neoadjuvant strategy — what tumor board debates

  • Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT): deliver all chemoradiation and systemic chemo before surgery. Improves distant control and downstaging, raises pCR/cCR. OPRA showed consolidation chemo after chemoradiation preserves the rectum in ~half of selected patients.
  • Watch-and-wait / NOM: patients with a clinical complete response (cCR) after TNT may be surveilled instead of resected — DRE + flexible endoscopy + MRI intensively (scope every 3–4 months, MRI ~every 6 months early), salvage surgery if regrowth. Builds on Habr-Gama & the International Watch & Wait Database.
  • PROSPECT (de-escalation of radiation): for intermediate-risk rectal cancer (cT2N+, cT3N0, cT3N+) eligible for sphincter-sparing surgery, neoadjuvant FOLFOX with selective chemoradiation was non-inferior to standard chemoradiation; only ~9–10% needed radiation. Excluded T4, N2, and tumors ≤3 mm from the mesorectal fascia — does not apply to high-risk disease.
  • dMMR locally advanced rectal — the headline shift: neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade (dostarlimab) produced cCR approaching 100% in the landmark cohort, durable over years, patients avoiding chemoradiation and surgery. Now in NCCN guidance; trials (AZUR-1) extending it. If a rectal patient is routed to med-onc instead of the OR, this is often why.

ctDNA / minimal residual disease — the emerging tool

  • ctDNA detects MRD after curative resection. Post-op positivity carries a markedly higher recurrence risk (HR often 5–10×), outperforms CEA, with lead times up to ~11 months before radiographic recurrence.
  • De-escalation is better supported than escalation: DYNAMIC showed a ctDNA-negative result can safely spare chemo in stage II; whether intensifying chemo rescues ctDNA-positive patients is still unproven (CIRCULATE, COBRA, DYNAMIC-III validating). Increasingly ordered — recognize it on a chart, but don't treat ctDNA-positive escalation as settled. (UCSD's ORACLE observational trial is enrolling here.)
05

Perioperative / medical management

Your daily bread — ERAS, ileus, SSI, VTE, fluids
▾

ERAS — colorectal is where it was born

  • Preop: structured counseling, carbohydrate loading, avoid prolonged fasting (clears up to 2 h, solids up to 6 h), no routine long-acting sedative premed, immunonutrition for malnourished, VTE/antibiotic prophylaxis timing.
  • Bowel prep: trend favors mechanical bowel prep + oral antibiotics (neomycin/erythromycin or neomycin/metronidazole) for elective colorectal to cut SSI and possibly leak; "no prep" used selectively. Don't be surprised by either.
  • Intraop: MIS when feasible, goal-directed (euvolemic) fluids, avoid hypotension (MAP ≥65), normothermia, multimodal/regional analgesia (TAP block, epidural for open), avoid routine NG tubes & drains.
  • Postop: early oral feeding (don't wait for flatus), early mobilization (OOB POD0–1), opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia, early Foley/IV removal, VTE prophylaxis, alvimopan to speed bowel recovery, gum chewing as a cheap motility nudge.
  • Why you care: most "orders" you write are ERAS elements — knowing the pathway lets you anticipate the next step.

Ileus vs mechanical obstruction

  • Ileus (expected, functional): diffuse quiet bowel sounds, gradual distension, nausea, diffuse gas pattern. Worsened by opioids, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, immobility, inflammation. Manage: minimize opioids, correct electrolytes, mobilize, alvimopan, NG if vomiting.
  • Early mechanical SBO: colicky pain, transition point on CT, often adhesive or internal hernia / twisted stoma limb. Different problem; may need intervention.
  • Both look like "distended, not passing gas, nauseated" — the team leans on exam, electrolytes, and CT to separate them.

SSI & post-op diarrhea

  • SSI is among the most common complications (why MBP + oral antibiotics trend). Superficial incisional: open the wound, pack, ± antibiotics for surrounding cellulitis. Deep / organ-space (intra-abdominal/pelvic abscess): image-guided percutaneous drainage or return to OR + antibiotics — always raises the question of an underlying anastomotic leak.
  • Post-op C. diff: new watery diarrhea, especially after perioperative antibiotics → send stool C. diff toxin/PCR; first-line is oral vancomycin (or fidaxomicin), not metronidazole, for all but the mildest cases.

VTE prophylaxis & fluids/nutrition

  • VTE: malignancy + pelvic surgery + immobility = high risk. Inpatient pharmacologic (LMWH/heparin) + mechanical (SCDs). Extended prophylaxis (~4 weeks of LMWH after discharge) is standard for abdominopelvic cancer resections.
  • New ileostomy / high output → track Na, K, Mg, HCO3, Cr daily; replete Mg liberally. Watch the POD2–3 mobilization diuresis after third-spacing.
  • Albumin/prealbumin, recent steroid/biologic exposure, smoking are modifiable leak/wound-healing risks — flag in IBD and cancer patients. Severe malnutrition may warrant preoperative optimization or delayed anastomosis.
06

The anastomotic leak

The single most important thing on this rotation
▾

Recognize it early — what the team is quietly watching for

  • Incidence ~1–30% by location — low rectal/coloanal leak the most, right-sided/ileocolic the least.
  • Classic timing: POD 5–7 (can be earlier, or present as late as 2–3 weeks).
  • First sign is often tachycardia and a clinical "something's off" before frank peritonitis. Then: fever, leukocytosis, rising CRP/procalcitonin, failure to progress / new or persistent ileus, increasing pain, purulent or feculent drain output, and the telltale patient who was improving then plateaus/backslides around POD 5. A CRP that fails to fall by ~POD 3–4 is a useful early red flag.
  • Many signs are neither sensitive nor specific, and a diverting ileostomy can mask the presentation — keep suspicion high.
  • Workup (stable): triple-contrast CT (oral + rectal + IV) of abdomen/pelvis — rectal contrast is key for low anastomoses — plus labs and exam.

Risk factors

  • Non-modifiable: low (rectal) anastomosis / short distance from verge, male sex / narrow pelvis, emergency surgery, comorbidity, prior pelvic radiation.
  • Modifiable: malnutrition/hypoalbuminemia, steroids/immunosuppression, smoking, obesity, perioperative transfusion, possibly NSAIDs.
  • Intraoperative: tension (mitigated by splenic flexure mobilization), poor perfusion (assessed by ICG), contamination, technical factors. Higher-volume surgeons and stapled ileocolic anastomoses leak less.

Management & the classic teaching points

  • Management ranges from antibiotics ± percutaneous drainage (contained leak, stable) to reoperation with washout + proximal diversion or takedown (free leak, peritonitis, sepsis). Delay in recognition directly increases mortality.
  • The diverting loop ileostomy mitigates the consequences of a leak (less peritonitis/sepsis, fewer reoperations, lower mortality) — it does NOT prevent the leak itself. A classic, frequent pimp question.
  • The NSAID debate: non-selective NSAIDs (especially ketorolac) associated in some studies with higher leak rates; practice varies. Know it's debated, not settled.

Benign & inflammatory disease

IBD · diverticulitis · ischemic colitis · C. diff · anorectal

IBD surgery — UC vs Crohn's is a core distinction

Ulcerative colitis Crohn's disease
PatternMucosal, continuous, rectum proximally, colon onlyTransmural, skip lesions, mouth-to-anus
Surgery intentCurative (remove colon + rectum)Palliative — resect problem, preserve length
Hallmark complicationsToxic megacolon, dysplasia/cancer, bleedingFistulas, abscesses, strictures, perianal disease
Definitive opTotal proctocolectomy ± IPAASegmental resection / strictureplasty
  • Acute severe UC (ASUC): diagnosed by Truelove & Witts criteria. Treat with IV corticosteroids; no response in ~3–5 days → rescue infliximab or cyclosporine; ongoing failure, perforation, refractory bleeding, or toxic megacolon → surgery.
  • Emergency UC surgery = subtotal/total abdominal colectomy with end ileostomy — NOT a pouch. Sick patients on high-dose steroids have far higher pouch complication rates and the diagnosis may be indeterminate. Rectum and pouch dealt with later.
  • IPAA staging — 2-stage (standard elective): (1) proctocolectomy + pouch + diverting loop ileostomy, (2) ileostomy reversal ~8–12 weeks later. 3-stage (sick/steroids/emergency/toxic megacolon): (1) TAC + end ileostomy, (2) completion proctectomy + pouch + diverting ileostomy, (3) reversal.
  • Crohn's caution: generally a contraindication to IPAA (high failure from fistulizing/recurrent disease), though selected Crohn's-colitis patients are offered pouches at expert centers. Many "pouch failures" labeled Crohn's are actually mechanical problems fixable with redo pouch surgery.
  • Toxic megacolon: colonic dilation (>6 cm) + systemic toxicity; complicates UC or C. diff. Surgical emergency if it doesn't reverse → TAC with end ileostomy.
  • Pouchitis: most common long-term IPAA complication; treat with antibiotics (ciprofloxacin/metronidazole). Recurrent/refractory raises suspicion for Crohn's of the pouch. ~10–15% of colitis is "indeterminate."
  • Perioperative biologics/steroids: high-dose corticosteroids raise complication/infection risk — a major reason emergency/steroid-dependent patients get staged operations with diversion. Data on anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab are mixed/reassuring, but timing relative to surgery and steroid taper is a routine multidisciplinary discussion.

Diverticulitis — Hinchey & modern management

  • Modified Hinchey: Ia confined pericolic inflammation/phlegmon; Ib pericolic/mesocolic abscess; II walled-off pelvic/retroperitoneal/distant abscess; III purulent peritonitis; IV feculent peritonitis (free perforation).
  • Diagnosis: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast (sensitivity ~94%, specificity ~99%).
  • Uncomplicated (Hinchey Ia): increasingly outpatient; oral antibiotics shorten stays, and selected immunocompetent patients can be managed without antibiotics (DINAMO and related RCTs). When used: ciprofloxacin + metronidazole or amoxicillin-clavulanate. Sigmoid is the usual site (~60–70%); right-sided predominates in Asian populations.
  • Abscess (Ib/II): percutaneous drainage when ≥~4 cm + IV antibiotics (pip-tazo or cipro/metronidazole); smaller abscesses often resolve on antibiotics alone. Failure to improve within ~48 h → escalate to surgery.
  • Perforated (III/IV): resection. Hartmann classically for unstable/feculent contamination, but primary resection with anastomosis (± diverting loop ileostomy) is increasingly preferred in selected stable patients — lower overall morbidity, higher eventual reversal rates. Laparoscopic lavage a bridging option in select Hinchey III.
  • Follow-up colonoscopy (6–8 weeks) after complicated diverticulitis to exclude malignancy; no longer routine after an uncomplicated first episode (malignancy yield ~2–3%). Elective sigmoidectomy is individualized (recurrence, complications, fistula like colovesical → pneumaturia/fecaluria, stricture), not a fixed episode count.

Ischemic colitis & C. diff (surgical angle)

  • Ischemic colitis: lives at the watershed zones (Griffiths, Sudeck). Older patient, low-flow/hypotensive event, bloody diarrhea + crampy pain, "thumbprinting" (submucosal edema) on imaging. Rectum usually spared (dual supply). Mostly medical (bowel rest, IV fluids, antibiotics); surgery for full-thickness necrosis, perforation, peritonitis. Distinguish from acute mesenteric ischemia (SMA event, "pain out of proportion," small bowel, surgical emergency).
  • Fulminant C. diff (ileus, megacolon, shock, rising lactate, marked leukocytosis) failing maximal medical therapy → surgery: TAC with end ileostomy, or the colon-sparing diverting loop ileostomy + intraoperative colonic lavage with antegrade vancomycin.

Anorectal disease (clinic & minor OR)

  • Cryptoglandular hypothesis: obstruction/infection of anal glands at the dentate line is the common origin of abscess → fistula.
  • Anorectal abscess: treat with incision and drainage (don't wait it out; antibiotics alone insufficient). Add antibiotics for cellulitis, immunosuppression, diabetes, valvular disease. Named by space: perianal, ischiorectal, intersphincteric, supralevator.
  • Fistula-in-ano (Parks): intersphincteric, transsphincteric, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric. Goodsall's rule: external opening anterior to the transverse anal line → straight radial tract; posterior (or anterior >3 cm away) → curved tract to the posterior midline. Treatment: fistulotomy for low/simple; seton (draining/cutting), LIFT, or advancement flap for complex/high tracts to protect continence. Perianal Crohn's: drain sepsis, place setons, then medical therapy (anti-TNF) first.
  • Anal fissure: posterior midline tear (lateral/multiple suggest Crohn's, HIV, TB, malignancy); severe pain with defecation + bright-red blood; driven by internal sphincter hypertonia/ischemia. Medical first: fiber, sitz baths, topical nitroglycerin or CCB (nifedipine/diltiazem), botulinum toxin; lateral internal sphincterotomy for refractory (small incontinence risk).
  • Hemorrhoids: internal graded I–IV (I bleed/no prolapse, II prolapse-reduce spontaneously, III need manual reduction, IV irreducible). Rubber band ligation grades I–III; hemorrhoidectomy grade III–IV or mixed; stapled hemorrhoidopexy alternative for II–III. Thrombosed external: excise if presenting within ~72 h, else supportive.
  • Pilonidal disease, rectal prolapse (rectopexy ± resection; vs internal prolapse), and pelvic floor / defecatory dysfunction also flow through this service.
  • Anal cancer (squamous, HPV-related) is treated with chemoradiation (Nigro protocol), not primary surgery — APR is salvage. Don't reflexively assume an anal lesion goes to the OR first.

Things you maybe didn't realize were relevant

LARS · ureteral stents · presacral bleeding · hereditary syndromes
▾

Quality-of-life & intraoperative dread

  • Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS): after sphincter-preserving rectal surgery — urgency, frequency, clustering, fragmentation, incontinence. A major QoL burden and part of why organ-preserving strategies are attractive. Has a name and a validated LARS score.
  • Prophylactic ureteral stents: urology often places stents before redo pelvic, large tumor, or severe diverticular cases because the ureters get encased in inflammation. Postop stents = that's why; watch urine output and for hematuria.
  • Presacral venous bleeding: dreaded complication during low rectal/TME dissection — the presacral venous plexus is valveless, high-volume, very hard to control.
  • Perineal wound after APR / pelvic exenteration: large, slow-healing, sometimes flap-reconstructed (e.g., VRAM) — an ongoing source of morbidity to track.
  • ICG fluorescence angiography: inject indocyanine green to assess anastomotic perfusion intraoperatively — a leak risk you can sometimes see in the OR.

Hereditary syndromes you should recognize

  • Why CEA, why MMR, why genetics: not checkbox labs. A young patient, right-sided or multiple tumors, and dMMR → Lynch workup that changes the whole family's care.
  • Lynch (HNPCC): germline MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, or EPCAM. Identified mainly by universal tumor MMR/MSI testing (old Amsterdam II / Bethesda criteria miss ~half). Surveillance colonoscopy every 1–2 years. When CRC develops, many advocate extended/subtotal colectomy (high metachronous risk). Aspirin reduces CRC risk (CAPP2). Endometrial cancer is the #2 malignancy — extracolonic surveillance matters.
  • Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP): germline APC, hundreds–thousands of adenomas, near-100% cancer risk → prophylactic colectomy/proctocolectomy (TAC-IRA or proctocolectomy-IPAA) typically in late teens/20s. Plus upper GI/duodenal surveillance (ampullary/duodenal cancer) and desmoid tumor risk (can preclude pouch).
  • MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP): autosomal recessive (biallelic MUTYH), attenuated-polyposis phenotype — the recessive mimic of FAP.
  • Synchronous (3–5%) & metachronous cancers: the reason for full preoperative colonoscopy and lifelong surveillance.

Drains & readmissions

  • Drains tell stories: know each drain's volume and character daily. Feculent/purulent or a sudden change around POD 5 = think leak.
  • Why the loop-ileostomy patient bounces back in a month: dehydration from high output → AKI → readmission, not the incision. Counsel hydration + loperamide before discharge.

How to be useful

Earn a good eval without loving the OR
▾
  • Prerounds checklist per patient: POD#, operation + indication, vitals and trends (especially HR), I&Os, stoma & drain outputs (volume + character), flatus/stool, diet status, lines/tubes/Foley, pain regimen (and opioid-sparing progress), ambulation, key labs (WBC, lytes, Cr, Mg, Hgb, lactate if sick), antibiotics + day, VTE prophylaxis.
  • Present in surgical cadence — tight and forward-looking: "POD3 from sigmoidectomy for a diverticular stricture, afebrile, HR 70s–80s, tolerating regular diet, ostomy 600 mL over 24h, passing flatus, ambulating, pain controlled on PO. Plan: Foley out, transition fully to PO analgesia, continue ERAS, anticipate discharge POD4–5."
  • In the OR: know today's relevant anatomy (TME plane, ureter, vascular pedicle), ask one good thinking question, otherwise retract well and watch the planes. You don't have to perform enthusiasm.
  • The data the team cares about most: trending heart rate, stoma/drain output, return of bowel function, and whether the patient is progressing or backsliding.
  • Be the student who flags the POD5 tachycardia. Anticipating the leak conversation is worth a month of forced enthusiasm.

Daily routine, logistics & discharge criteria

The day · what to carry · how to be useful
▾

The day

  • Arrive early (~5:00–5:30 AM) to preround. Surgery runs ahead of the clock; being late once is remembered.
  • Preround data per patient: overnight events, vitals + trends (especially HR), I&Os, drain outputs (volume + character), stoma output (color, volume), labs (CBC, BMP, ± lactate), wound/incision check, and ask the patient directly about pain, flatus/BM, nausea, ambulation, diet tolerance. Have your note/card ready before rounds.
  • Rounding presentation (surgical cadence): one-liner → overnight events → vitals/I&Os → focused exam → labs → assessment + plan toward discharge. For post-ops, lead with POD# + procedure and frame everything around discharge criteria.

Discharge criteria (ERAS targets)

Tolerating diet, passing flatus/BM, pain controlled on PO meds, ambulating independently, afebrile, and (if present) stoma functioning with acceptable output. Don't discharge a fresh ileostomy until output is controlled — high output is the top readmission driver.

What to carry & how to be genuinely useful

  • Carry: trauma shears, penlight, stethoscope, a pocket reference, a couple of pens. Knowing where to find op notes, pathology, and imaging quickly in Epic is its own skill — set up your patient lists and reports ahead of time.
  • Offer to help with dressing changes, drain management, and stoma care. See and present consults proactively. Read about tomorrow's cases tonight. Know your patients cold — labs, imaging, pathology. When unsure, go examine the patient.

OR etiquette & technical basics

Before the case · in the room · sutures & knots
▾

Before the case

  • Review the case the night before: history, imaging, pathology, and the planned procedure + relevant anatomy (the plane, the vessels, the ureter for that operation).
  • Know whether it's open, lap, or robotic, and what your likely role is.

In the room

  • Introduce yourself to the scrub tech and circulating nurse — they run the room and will help you. Ask where you can stand and what you can touch.
  • Scrub in early. Keep your hands above your waist and in front of you; assume everything below the waist and behind you is non-sterile.
  • Assisting: hold retractors steadily (constant, quiet tension — don't drift), follow the camera smoothly if driving the laparoscope, and suction where the surgeon is looking, not where you want to.
  • Cutting sutures: cut to a ~3 mm tail unless told otherwise (shorter for fine/deep sutures, longer for a planned tie-back). Cut with the tips, flat to the suture, and pause for the "cut" cue.
  • Know your knots: be able to two-hand tie and instrument tie competently; practice on any spare suture. Volunteering "can I throw a few?" at the right moment earns reps.
  • Anticipate, don't narrate. Watch the field, have the next move ready; ask one good question per case rather than a running commentary.
