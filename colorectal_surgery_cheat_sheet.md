## 0. Decode the alphabet soup (read this first)

A plain-English glossary of the abbreviations, jargon, and shorthand used on this service and throughout this sheet. Skim it once, then the rest will read cleanly.

### Ward and presentation shorthand
- **POD#** — postoperative day number (POD0 = day of surgery). The first thing you state for any post-op patient.
- **Pre-rounding** — seeing/charting your patients alone *before* team rounds so you can present them.
- **One-liner** — the single summary sentence: age, relevant history, POD#, operation, why they're here.
- **SOAP note** — Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan (the standard progress-note format).
- **Pimping** — rapid-fire questioning by attendings/residents to test (and teach) your knowledge.
- **Scut** — the non-glamorous task work (dressing changes, paperwork, chasing results); doing it well earns goodwill.
- **NPO** — *nil per os*, nothing by mouth.
- **I&O (I/Os)** — intake and output (fluids in vs urine/drain/stoma out); a core daily number.
- **DRE** — digital rectal exam.
- **CBC / BMP / CMP** — complete blood count / basic metabolic panel / comprehensive metabolic panel (labs).
- **WBC, Hgb, Cr, Mg, lactate** — white cells, hemoglobin, creatinine (kidney), magnesium, lactate (perfusion/sepsis marker).

### Anatomy and vessels
- **SMA / IMA** — superior / inferior mesenteric artery (the two main colon arteries).
- **SMV / IMV** — superior / inferior mesenteric vein.
- **SRA** — superior rectal artery; **TI** — terminal ileum.
- **TME** — total mesorectal excision (the key rectal-cancer dissection).
- **CME / CVL** — complete mesocolic excision / central vascular ligation (the colon-cancer equivalent).
- **CRM** — circumferential (radial) resection margin; **MRF** — mesorectal fascia; **EMVI** — extramural vascular invasion (all rectal-MRI/path terms).
- **IAS / EAS** — internal / external anal sphincter.
- **Dentate (pectinate) line** — the anatomic dividing line of the anal canal (epithelium, nerves, blood supply all change here).

### Operations and procedures
- **LAR** — low anterior resection (rectal cancer, sphincter-preserving).
- **APR** — abdominoperineal resection (removes anus → permanent colostomy).
- **TAC** — total abdominal colectomy; **IRA** — ileorectal anastomosis.
- **IPAA** — ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (the "J-pouch").
- **Hartmann** — resection + end colostomy + closed rectal stump (no anastomosis).
- **TaTME** — transanal TME; **TAMIS / TEM** — transanal minimally invasive surgery / transanal endoscopic microsurgery (local rectal excision).
- **EMR / ESD** — endoscopic mucosal resection / submucosal dissection (scope-based removal of early lesions).
- **LIFT** — ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract; **LIS** — lateral internal sphincterotomy (for fissures).
- **I&D** — incision and drainage (for abscess).
- **CRS / HIPEC** — cytoreductive surgery / hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (for peritoneal spread).
- **ICG** — indocyanine green (fluorescent dye to check bowel perfusion intraoperatively).
- **Brooke ileostomy** — the standard spouted ileostomy; **end vs loop** — single-lumen vs diverting two-opening stoma.

### Cancer staging and pathology
- **CRC / LARC / mCRC** — colorectal cancer / locally advanced rectal cancer / metastatic CRC.
- **TNM** — Tumor (depth), Node, Metastasis staging system; **AJCC** — the body that publishes it.
- **CEA** — carcinoembryonic antigen (blood tumor marker for trending, not screening).
- **pCR / cCR / MPR** — pathologic / clinical complete response / major pathologic response (how well a tumor melts away with pre-op therapy).
- **LVI / PNI** — lymphovascular / perineural invasion (high-risk path features).
- **IHC / NGS** — immunohistochemistry / next-generation sequencing (how tumors get molecularly profiled).

### Molecular markers and genes
- **MMR** — mismatch repair (a DNA-repair system); **dMMR / pMMR** — deficient / proficient.
- **MSI / MSI-H / MSS** — microsatellite instability / high / stable (the genomic signature of dMMR).
- **ctDNA** — circulating tumor DNA (tumor DNA in the blood); **MRD** — minimal/molecular residual disease.
- **KRAS / NRAS / BRAF / PIK3CA / HER2** — tumor mutations/markers that guide targeted drugs.
- **APC, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM, MUTYH** — genes behind hereditary CRC syndromes.
- **EGFR** — epidermal growth factor receptor (target of cetuximab/panitumumab).
- **PD-1 / PD-L1 / CTLA-4** — immune checkpoints blocked by immunotherapy drugs.
- **Lynch / HNPCC, FAP, MAP** — hereditary nonpolyposis CRC (Lynch) / familial adenomatous polyposis / MUTYH-associated polyposis.

### Chemo, drugs, and immunotherapy
- **5-FU** — 5-fluorouracil (backbone chemo); **leucovorin** — folinic acid (potentiates 5-FU); **capecitabine** — oral 5-FU prodrug.
- **FOLFOX / mFOLFOX6** — 5-FU + leucovorin + **ox**aliplatin (modified standard version).
- **CAPOX / XELOX** — **cap**ecitabine + **ox**aliplatin (same idea, oral fluoropyrimidine).
- **FOLFIRI** — 5-FU + leucovorin + **iri**notecan; **FOLFIRINOX** — adds both oxaliplatin and irinotecan.
- **Oxaliplatin** — platinum agent; signature toxicity is cumulative **neuropathy.**
- **ICI** — immune checkpoint inhibitor. Examples: **nivolumab, pembrolizumab, dostarlimab, atezolizumab** (anti-PD-1/PD-L1); **ipilimumab** (anti-CTLA-4).
- **Anti-EGFR** — cetuximab, panitumumab (only work in RAS wild-type, left-sided tumors).
- **Bevacizumab** — anti-VEGF (anti-angiogenesis) antibody.

### Treatment-strategy terms
- **Neoadjuvant** — therapy *before* surgery; **adjuvant** — therapy *after* surgery.
- **TNT** — total neoadjuvant therapy (all chemo + radiation given before surgery).
- **CRT / SCRT / LCRT** — chemoradiation / short-course RT / long-course chemoradiation.
- **W&W / NOM** — watch-and-wait / non-operative management (skip surgery if the tumor fully responds).
- **DFS / OS / PFS / RFS** — disease-free / overall / progression-free / recurrence-free survival (trial endpoints).
- **NCCN** — National Comprehensive Cancer Network (writes the cancer-treatment guidelines).

### Perioperative, complications, and management
- **ERAS** — Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (the standardized perioperative pathway you'll round inside).
- **MBP** — mechanical bowel prep; **ORS** — oral rehydration solution.
- **VTE / DVT / PE** — venous thromboembolism / deep vein thrombosis / pulmonary embolism.
- **LMWH** — low-molecular-weight heparin; **SCD** — sequential compression device (the leg sleeves).
- **SSI** — surgical site infection; **AKI** — acute kidney injury.
- **SBO / LBO** — small / large bowel obstruction; **ileus** — sluggish, non-mechanical bowel (functional).
- **NG tube** — nasogastric tube (decompresses the stomach); **Foley** — urinary catheter.
- **TAP block** — transversus abdominis plane block (regional analgesia for the abdominal wall).
- **MAP** — mean arterial pressure (kept ≥65 intraop); **PONV** — post-op nausea and vomiting; **LOS** — length of stay; **ASA class** — anesthesia risk grade.
- **Alvimopan** — drug that speeds return of bowel function; **loperamide** — anti-diarrheal for high stoma output.

### Disease and benign-condition terms
- **IBD / UC / CD** — inflammatory bowel disease / ulcerative colitis / Crohn's disease; **ASUC** — acute severe UC.
- **Anti-TNF** — anti-tumor-necrosis-factor biologic (e.g., infliximab).
- **LARS** — low anterior resection syndrome (altered bowel habits after rectal surgery).
- **Hinchey** — the severity grading for complicated diverticulitis (I–IV).
- **Goodsall's rule** — predicts the path of an anal fistula tract.
- **C. diff** — *Clostridioides difficile* (the post-antibiotic diarrhea/colitis bug).
- **HPV** — human papillomavirus (cause of anal squamous cancer).

### Trials you'll hear name-dropped (one-line decoder)
- **PROSPECT** — showed selected rectal cancers can skip radiation (FOLFOX + selective chemoradiation).
- **OPRA / RAPIDO / PRODIGE 23** — established total neoadjuvant therapy and organ preservation in rectal cancer.
- **CheckMate 8HW / KEYNOTE-177** — immunotherapy first-line for metastatic dMMR CRC.
- **ATOMIC** — adjuvant atezolizumab + FOLFOX for stage III dMMR colon cancer.
- **NICHE-2 / NICHE-3** — neoadjuvant immunotherapy for dMMR colon cancer.
- **BREAKWATER** — first-line encorafenib + cetuximab + chemo for BRAF V600E metastatic CRC.
- **DYNAMIC / CIRCULATE / ORACLE** — ctDNA-guided adjuvant-therapy trials (ORACLE enrolls at UCSD).
- **IDEA** — defined 3- vs 6-month adjuvant chemo duration.

## 1. Anatomy you will actually be asked about

### Segments, fixation, and surface anatomy
- **Order:** cecum → ascending → hepatic flexure → transverse → splenic flexure → descending → sigmoid → rectum (12–15 cm) → anal canal (~2–5 cm).
- **Fixation (dictates what gets mobilized):** **ascending and descending colon are retroperitoneal** (fixed); **transverse and sigmoid are intraperitoneal** on a mesentery (mobile). This is why the flexures must be **taken down/mobilized** to gain reach for an anastomosis, and why the **mobile sigmoid and cecum** are the segments that **volvulize**.
- **Taeniae coli:** three longitudinal muscle bands that **converge at the appendiceal base** (a landmark for finding the appendix) and again at the **rectosigmoid junction**, where they fan out into the **continuous longitudinal muscle layer of the rectum** (the rectum has no taeniae, haustra, or appendices epiploicae).

### Arterial supply, by embryologic origin
- **Midgut → SMA:** cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, proximal 2/3 of the transverse colon. Branches: **ileocolic** (most consistent, supplies cecum/TI), **right colic** (variable, often absent), **middle colic**.
- **Hindgut → IMA:** distal 1/3 transverse, descending colon, sigmoid, upper rectum. Branches: **left colic**, **sigmoidal branches**, terminating as the **superior rectal (superior hemorrhoidal) artery**.
- **Why oncologic resections look the way they do:** you take the whole vascular pedicle plus its draining lymphatics. Right-sided cancer → ligate ileocolic ± right/middle colic (right hemicolectomy). Left-sided → high IMA ligation. The resection margin is dictated by blood supply and lymphatic drainage, not just the tumor edge. A minimum of **12 lymph nodes** should be examined for accurate staging.

### Collaterals and watershed zones (high-yield for ischemia)
- **Marginal artery of Drummond:** continuous collateral arcade along the mesenteric border connecting SMA and IMA territories. When present and robust, it perfuses the colon after a pedicle is ligated.
- **Arc of Riolan (meandering mesenteric artery):** a more central, variable SMA–IMA collateral. Becomes prominent when there's proximal SMA or IMA stenosis.
- **Griffiths point (splenic flexure):** watershed between middle colic (SMA) and left colic (IMA). #1 site of ischemic colitis.
- **Sudeck point (rectosigmoid junction):** watershed between last sigmoidal branch and superior rectal artery.
- **Clinical payoff:** after high IMA ligation for a left-sided cancer, distal colonic perfusion hinges on the marginal artery via the SMA. A poor marginal artery = a tenuous, leak-prone anastomosis. This is one reason surgeons **mobilize the splenic flexure** — to get a well-perfused, tension-free reach.

### Venous and lymphatic drainage (explains metastatic patterns)
- Colon and **upper rectum** → portal system (SMV/IMV → portal vein) → **liver is the first and most common met site.**
- **Lower rectum/anal canal** → partly systemic via middle/inferior rectal veins → internal iliac → IVC, so low lesions can metastasize to **lung directly, bypassing the liver.**
- Lymphatics follow arteries. Above the dentate line → internal iliac/mesenteric nodes. Below → **inguinal nodes** (why anal canal cancers get groin attention).

### Rectum and the surgical planes
- Rectum ~12–15 cm from the anal verge; three transverse folds (valves of Houston). No true mesentery — it carries the **mesorectum**, a fascial envelope of fat, vessels, and lymphatics.
- **Total mesorectal excision (TME)** = sharp dissection in the avascular areolar "holy plane" between mesorectal fascia (visceral) and presacral/parietal fascia. Sharp TME (Heald) dramatically reduced local recurrence vs old blunt techniques.
- **Circumferential resection margin (CRM):** the radial margin. A positive or threatened CRM (≤1 mm) is the strongest predictor of local recurrence and a key reason for neoadjuvant therapy. Pelvic MRI is used pre-op specifically to assess the relationship of tumor to the mesorectal fascia.
- Rectal arterial supply: **superior rectal** (IMA), **middle rectal** (internal iliac), **inferior rectal** (internal pudendal from internal iliac).

### Anal canal — the dentate line is the whole exam

| Feature | Above dentate line | Below dentate line |
|---|---|---|
| Embryology | Endoderm (hindgut) | Ectoderm (proctodeum) |
| Epithelium | Columnar → transitional | Squamous |
| Innervation | Visceral (insensate) | Somatic, inferior rectal n. (pain) |
| Arterial | Superior/middle rectal | Inferior rectal |
| Venous/lymphatic | Portal → internal iliac nodes | Systemic → inguinal nodes |
| Hemorrhoids | Internal (painless, bleed) | External (painful when thrombosed) |
| Malignancy | Adenocarcinoma | Squamous cell carcinoma |

- You can band internal hemorrhoids in clinic (visceral, insensate) but never an external one (somatic, exquisitely painful).
- **Anal transition zone** sits just above the dentate line — relevant in IPAA (a small cuff left behind can develop "cuffitis" or dysplasia).

### Sphincter complex and continence
- **Internal anal sphincter (IAS):** smooth muscle, involuntary, autonomic. Continuation of the rectal **circular** muscle. Provides most resting tone.
- **External anal sphincter (EAS):** skeletal muscle, voluntary (pudendal n.). Continuous superiorly with the **puborectalis** and levator ani.
- **Puborectalis sling** maintains the anorectal angle — key to gross continence. The palpable upper edge of the sphincter complex on DRE is the **anorectal ring**; dividing it causes incontinence (limits how aggressive a fistulotomy can be).

### Pelvic autonomic nerves (the ones surgeons protect)
- **Superior hypogastric plexus / hypogastric nerves (sympathetic, L1–L3):** ejaculation, bladder neck closure. Injury → **retrograde ejaculation**, bladder dysfunction. At risk at the IMA origin and sacral promontory.
- **Pelvic splanchnic nerves / inferior hypogastric (nervi erigentes) plexus (parasympathetic, S2–S4):** erection, detrusor contraction. Injury → **erectile and bladder dysfunction.** At risk along the lateral pelvic sidewalls during low dissection.
- "Nerve-sparing TME" is the deliberate preservation of these planes — a major quality-of-life issue, especially in younger patients.

## 2. The operations — decode the eponyms and know the why

- **Right hemicolectomy:** cecum/ascending ± hepatic flexure; ileocolic anastomosis. Generally lower leak risk (small bowel to colon, excellent blood supply; stapled ileocolic anastomoses leak less than handsewn per meta-analysis). **Extended right** adds the transverse colon for hepatic flexure/proximal transverse lesions.
- **Left hemicolectomy / sigmoidectomy:** descending/sigmoid; colocolonic or colorectal anastomosis. Higher leak risk than right-sided.
- **Low anterior resection (LAR):** rectal cancer with **sphincter preservation.** TME + colorectal or coloanal anastomosis. Low anastomoses (<5 cm from anal verge), coloanal anastomoses, and IPAA are usually **protected by a diverting loop ileostomy.**
- **Abdominoperineal resection (APR):** very low rectal/anal cancers where the sphincter can't be spared. Removes rectum **and** anus → **permanent end colostomy.** The **perineal wound** is large and slow-healing (sometimes flap-reconstructed) — watch it on rounds.
- **Hartmann procedure:** segmental (usually sigmoid/rectal) resection with **end colostomy + oversewn distal/rectal stump**, no anastomosis. Classic for perforated diverticulitis or obstructing left-sided cancer in a contaminated/unstable field. "Reversal" later is a real, sometimes difficult second operation, and a meaningful fraction are never reversed.
- **Total abdominal colectomy (TAC):** entire colon removed; ileorectal anastomosis (elective) or **end ileostomy** (emergent/sick). Indications: UC, fulminant C. diff, synchronous tumors, attenuated FAP/Lynch, slow-transit constipation.
- **Total proctocolectomy:** colon + rectum + anus (with end ileostomy) or with **IPAA** (sphincter-preserving, below).
- **Restorative proctocolectomy with IPAA ("J-pouch"):** definitive operation for UC and FAP. Removes colon and rectum, builds an ileal reservoir anastomosed to the anus, restoring transanal defecation. Usually **staged** (see IBD section). Complications to memorize: **pouchitis** (most common long-term), anastomotic leak, pelvic sepsis, **cuffitis**, pouch fistula, and pouch failure.
- **TaTME (transanal TME):** bottom-up dissection for difficult low rectal tumors, obese/narrow male pelvis. Note the **local-recurrence controversy** — Norway paused TaTME nationally over early recurrence signals, so it's reserved for selected cases at experienced centers.
- **Local options:** **transanal excision / TAMIS / TEM** for select early (T1, favorable) rectal lesions; **polypectomy/EMR/ESD** endoscopically for early lesions.

**Technique concepts worth recognizing in the OR:**
- **Complete mesocolic excision (CME) / central vascular ligation (CVL):** the colon-cancer analogue of TME — dissect along embryologic planes and ligate the feeding vessel at its origin for a complete mesenteric envelope and maximal node yield.
- **Intracorporeal vs extracorporeal anastomosis:** in minimally invasive right colectomy, **intracorporeal** anastomosis (sewn/stapled inside the abdomen) is associated with faster bowel-function recovery and fewer wound/hernia issues than pulling the bowel out to anastomose.
- **ICG fluorescence angiography:** IV indocyanine green to **confirm anastomotic perfusion** intraoperatively (a modifiable leak risk you can sometimes see before committing).

At a referral center, expect a high proportion of **redo / reversal / completion** cases — Hartmann reversals, ileostomy takedowns, redo pouches, recurrent Crohn's resections, re-resections for recurrence. These mean dense adhesions, longer cases, and higher complication rates.

## 3. Stomas — students never understand these, attendings always ask

**Identify the stoma before rounds. Its location and construction tell you the operation.**

| | Ileostomy | Colostomy |
|---|---|---|
| Usual location | RLQ | LLQ |
| Construction | **Spouted** (Brooke spout protects skin from caustic enzyme-rich effluent) | Flush to skin |
| Output character | Liquid/loose, enzyme-rich | More formed, feculent |
| Typical output | High (often 800–1200+ mL/day) | Lower |

- **End vs loop:** *End* = single lumen, distal bowel removed or oversewn (e.g., APR, Hartmann, end ileostomy after TAC). *Loop* = two openings on one stoma, almost always **temporary/diverting** to protect a downstream anastomosis (e.g., loop ileostomy after LAR or IPAA).
- **Diverting loop ileostomy is preferred over loop transverse colostomy** for protecting low anastomoses — lower rates of prolapse, parastomal hernia, and reversal complications.
- **High ostomy output is the thing you'll actually manage.** A new ileostomy putting out **>1200–1500 mL/day** drives dehydration, **AKI, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic acidosis.** **Dehydration from high ostomy output is the leading cause of readmission after colorectal surgery.**
  - Management ladder: **anti-motility agents** (loperamide, often high-dose; add diphenoxylate-atropine), **bulking/thickening** (fiber, banana flakes), **oral rehydration solution** rather than free water (hypotonic fluid worsens sodium losses), **PPI/H2 blocker** to reduce secretory volume, hold diuretics/laxatives, **aggressively replete magnesium**, and somatostatin analogs in refractory cases.
- **Round on the number.** "Ileostomy put out 1,400 over 24 hours, making good urine, lytes stable, repleted Mg" is exactly the kind of line that signals competence.
- **Stoma viability:** pink/red = healthy; **dusky/black = ischemia → escalate now.** A small rim of necrosis above fascia may be watched; necrosis below fascia is an emergency.
- **A stoma that stops + distension + nausea = obstruction or ileus**, not "it's resting." Loop ileostomy "outlet obstruction" is a known early complication.
- **Parastomal hernia, prolapse, retraction, stenosis, and high-output dermatitis** are the chronic complications.

## 4. Colorectal oncology — the part that's changing fastest

### Workup and staging
- **Colon:** full colonoscopy with biopsy + **tattoo** of the lesion, **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis**, baseline **CEA.** Clear the whole colon (synchronous cancers in ~3–5%).
- **Rectal adds:** **pelvic MRI** (T stage, nodal status, CRM, EMVI) and often **endorectal ultrasound** for early lesions. Local staging drives neoadjuvant decisions.
- **AJCC 8th edition TNM (know the spine of it):**
  - **T:** Tis (intramucosal), T1 (submucosa), T2 (muscularis propria), T3 (through MP into pericolorectal tissue), T4a (penetrates visceral peritoneum), T4b (invades adjacent organ/structure).
  - **N:** N1 (1–3 nodes; N1c = **tumor deposits** with no positive nodes), N2 (≥4 nodes).
  - **M:** M1a (one organ/site), M1b (>1 organ), M1c (peritoneal).
  - **Rough stage map:** I = T1–2N0; II = T3–4N0; III = any N+; IV = M1.
- **CEA** is a surveillance/trend marker, **not screening.** A rising CEA after curative resection = recurrence until proven otherwise.
- **Screening & epidemiology (why younger patients show up):** **early-onset CRC (<50) is rising**, and average-risk screening now **starts at age 45** (ACS/USPSTF). Options include colonoscopy, FIT, stool-DNA, and the newer **blood-based cfDNA test (Shield)** — FDA-approved (2024) and NCCN-recognized (2025) for average-risk adults 45+, with colonoscopy still the gold standard and required for any positive non-colonoscopy test.
- **Stage IV / advanced surgical oncology (briefly):** **colorectal liver metastases** can be **resected for cure** when technically feasible, sometimes after **conversion chemotherapy** downsizes them — surgery has a real role in oligometastatic M1 disease. **Peritoneal metastases:** **cytoreductive surgery (CRS) ± HIPEC** at specialized centers, though **PRODIGE 7** questioned the added benefit of the HIPEC (mitomycin) component over complete cytoreduction alone.

### Molecular testing every tumor gets — and why it matters
- **Universal MMR/MSI testing** on all colorectal cancers (IHC for MLH1/MSH2/MSH6/PMS2, or PCR/NGS for microsatellite instability). Two reasons:
  1. **Lynch screening.** Loss of staining flags possible Lynch syndrome → germline testing + genetics referral. If **MLH1 is lost**, do reflex **BRAF V600E** and/or **MLH1 promoter methylation** testing — if positive, it's likely **sporadic** (methylation-driven), not Lynch.
  2. **Therapy and prognosis.** **dMMR/MSI-high tumors respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors** and **resist single-agent fluoropyrimidine** chemo. In stage II, dMMR is a **good** prognostic sign and argues *against* adjuvant 5-FU monotherapy.
- **RAS/RAF and targeted therapy in metastatic disease:**
  - **KRAS/NRAS mutations** → no benefit from **anti-EGFR** (cetuximab/panitumumab); those work only in **RAS wild-type.** Even in RAS wild-type, **right-sided (cecum→transverse) primaries respond poorly to anti-EGFR** — **tumor sidedness** guides first-line biologic choice (anti-EGFR favored for left-sided, bevacizumab for right-sided).
  - **BRAF V600E** (~10%, poor prognosis): **BREAKWATER** established first-line **encorafenib + cetuximab + mFOLFOX6**, roughly doubling median OS (~30 months) — FDA fully approved (2026). The targeted regimen is now first-line, not just salvage (older BEACON used encorafenib + cetuximab after progression).
  - **KRAS G12C:** sotorasib or adagrasib **+ cetuximab** (CodeBreaK 300) in later lines.
  - **HER2 amplification:** tucatinib + trastuzumab (MOUNTAINEER), trastuzumab-deruxtecan — actionable in RAS/BRAF wild-type.

### Adjuvant chemotherapy after colon resection
- **Stage III (node-positive): adjuvant chemo is standard** — **FOLFOX** (5-FU/leucovorin/oxaliplatin) or **CAPOX/XELOX** (capecitabine/oxaliplatin), giving roughly a **15% absolute survival benefit.**
- **Duration (IDEA collaboration):** **3 vs 6 months** is risk-adapted. Lower-risk stage III (T1–3, N1) can often do 3 months of CAPOX (less oxaliplatin neurotoxicity); higher-risk (T4 or N2) generally needs 6 months.
- **Oxaliplatin's signature toxicity:** cumulative, dose-dependent **peripheral sensory neuropathy** (and cold-induced dysesthesia) — the reason shorter courses are attractive.
- **Stage II:** chemo is **selective**, reserved for **high-risk features** — T4, <12 nodes harvested, poorly differentiated histology, **lymphovascular or perineural invasion**, obstruction or perforation at presentation. **dMMR stage II generally should NOT get 5-FU monotherapy.**

### Immunotherapy across the dMMR/MSI-high spectrum (~10–15% of CRC) — moving earlier
dMMR/MSI-high tumors are exquisitely checkpoint-inhibitor sensitive, and immunotherapy is marching from metastatic disease into the curative-intent setting:
- **Metastatic:** **nivolumab + ipilimumab** is now first-line (CheckMate 8HW) — 2-year PFS ~72% vs ~14% with chemo; **pembrolizumab** monotherapy (KEYNOTE-177) is the single-agent option. Chemo is comparatively ineffective here.
- **Stage III colon — adjuvant (ATOMIC, ASCO 2025 plenary):** adding **atezolizumab to FOLFOX** improved 3-year DFS to **~86% vs ~77%** (HR ~0.50, a ~50% reduction in recurrence/death) — practice-changing for dMMR stage III.
- **Locally advanced colon — neoadjuvant (NICHE-2 / NICHE-3):** a short course of **nivolumab + ipilimumab** (or nivo + relatlimab) before surgery gives **~98–99% pathologic response, ~68% pCR**, with NICHE-2 reporting **~100% 3-year DFS.** The open question: neoadjuvant immuno may be even better than adjuvant and allow **de-escalation of chemo and surgery.**
- **Rectal:** see dostarlimab below (organ preservation without chemoradiation or surgery).
- **Bottom line for the wards:** if a dMMR patient is going to medical oncology before (or instead of) the OR, immunotherapy is usually why — and the field is actively pushing it earlier.

### Rectal cancer neoadjuvant strategy — what tumor board is debating
- **Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT):** deliver **all** chemoradiation **and** systemic chemo **before** surgery. Improves distant control and downstaging, increases pathologic/clinical complete response. The **OPRA trial** showed consolidation chemo after chemoradiation preserves the rectum in ~half of selected patients.
- **Watch-and-wait / non-operative management:** patients achieving a **clinical complete response (cCR)** after TNT may be surveilled instead of resected — DRE + flexible endoscopy + MRI, intensively (scope every 3–4 months, MRI every ~6 months early on), with salvage surgery if regrowth. Builds on the Habr-Gama experience and the International Watch & Wait Database.
- **PROSPECT trial (de-escalation of radiation):** for **intermediate-risk** rectal cancer (cT2N+, cT3N0, cT3N+) eligible for sphincter-sparing surgery, **neoadjuvant FOLFOX with selective use of chemoradiation** was **non-inferior** to standard chemoradiation. Only ~9–10% needed radiation (those with <20% tumor regression). 5-year outcomes were excellent in both arms. **Excluded** T4, N2, and tumors threatening the mesorectal fascia (≤3 mm) — so it does **not** apply to high-risk disease. Takeaway: many intermediate-risk patients can now **avoid pelvic radiation** and its long-term toxicity.
- **dMMR locally advanced rectal cancer — the headline shift:** neoadjuvant **PD-1 blockade (dostarlimab)** produced **clinical complete response approaching 100%** in the landmark cohort, durable over years, with patients avoiding chemoradiation **and** surgery. Now incorporated into NCCN guidance for locally advanced dMMR rectal cancer; trials (e.g., AZUR-1) are extending this. ~10–15% of CRC is dMMR. If a rectal patient is being routed to medical oncology instead of the OR, **this is often why.**

### ctDNA / minimal residual disease — the emerging tool (good "didn't realize" topic)
- **Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)** detects **minimal residual disease (MRD)** after curative resection. Post-op ctDNA positivity carries a **markedly higher recurrence risk** (hazard ratios often 5–10x) and outperforms CEA, with **lead times of up to ~11 months** before radiographic recurrence.
- Trials (DYNAMIC, others) are testing **ctDNA-guided** adjuvant therapy. **The de-escalation use is better supported than the escalation use:** DYNAMIC showed a ctDNA-negative result can safely **spare chemo** in stage II, whereas whether **intensifying chemo rescues ctDNA-positive patients is still unproven** (the question CIRCULATE, COBRA, and DYNAMIC-III are validating). Increasingly ordered; worth recognizing on a chart, but don't treat ctDNA-positive escalation as settled. (UCSD's **ORACLE** observational ctDNA/MRD trial is enrolling here.)

## 5. Perioperative / medical management — your daily bread

### ERAS (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery) — colorectal is where ERAS was born
You'll round inside an ERAS pathway. Components worth naming, by phase:
- **Preop:** structured counseling/expectation-setting, **carbohydrate loading**, avoid prolonged fasting (**clear liquids up to 2 h, solids up to 6 h** before anesthesia), no routine long-acting sedative premed, **preoperative immunonutrition for malnourished patients**, VTE and antibiotic prophylaxis timing.
- **Bowel prep:** trend favors **mechanical bowel prep + oral antibiotics** (e.g., neomycin/erythromycin or neomycin/metronidazole) for elective colorectal to reduce SSI and possibly leak; "no prep" is used selectively. Don't be surprised by either approach.
- **Intraop:** minimally invasive (lap/robotic) when feasible, **goal-directed (euvolemic, not liberal) fluids** with **avoidance of hypotension (MAP ≥65)**, **normothermia**, multimodal/regional analgesia (**TAP block**, epidural for open), **avoid routine NG tubes and abdominal drains.**
- **Postop:** **early oral feeding** (don't wait for flatus), **early mobilization** (out of bed POD0–1), **opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia** (acetaminophen, NSAIDs where appropriate, regional), **early Foley and IV removal**, **VTE prophylaxis**, **alvimopan** (peripheral mu-opioid antagonist) to speed return of bowel function, and **gum chewing** as a cheap motility nudge.
- Why you care: most "orders" you write are ERAS elements. Knowing the pathway lets you anticipate the next step instead of reacting.

### Postoperative ileus vs mechanical obstruction — distinguish them
- **Ileus** (expected, functional): diffuse quiet/absent bowel sounds, gradual distension, nausea, **diffuse** gas pattern on imaging. Worsened by **opioids, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia**, immobility, intra-abdominal inflammation. Manage: minimize opioids, **correct electrolytes**, mobilize, alvimopan, NG decompression if vomiting.
- **Early mechanical SBO:** colicky pain, **transition point** on CT, often adhesive or from an internal hernia / twisted stoma limb. Different problem; may need intervention.
- Both look like "distended, not passing gas, nauseated." The team leans on exam, electrolyte correction, and CT to separate them.

### Surgical site infection (SSI) and post-op diarrhea
- **SSI** is among the most common complications after colorectal surgery (a major reason for the MBP + oral antibiotic trend).
  - **Superficial incisional:** open the wound, pack, local wound care ± antibiotics if surrounding cellulitis.
  - **Deep / organ-space (intra-abdominal/pelvic abscess):** **image-guided percutaneous drainage** or return to OR, plus antibiotics. An organ-space collection should always raise the question of an **underlying anastomotic leak.**
- **Post-op C. difficile:** new-onset **watery diarrhea**, especially after perioperative antibiotics → send **stool C. diff toxin/PCR**; first-line treatment is **oral vancomycin** (or fidaxomicin), not metronidazole, for all but the mildest cases. (Surgical C. diff is covered in §7.)

### VTE prophylaxis — colorectal cancer is high-risk
- Malignancy + pelvic surgery + immobility = high VTE risk. Inpatient pharmacologic prophylaxis (LMWH/heparin) + mechanical (SCDs). **Extended prophylaxis** (~4 weeks of LMWH after discharge) is standard for **abdominopelvic cancer resections.** Know each patient's regimen.

### Fluids, electrolytes, nutrition
- New ileostomy / high-output stoma → track **Na, K, Mg, HCO3, Cr** daily; replete Mg liberally.
- Watch the **POD2–3 mobilization diuresis** after initial third-spacing.
- **Albumin/prealbumin, recent steroid/biologic exposure, and smoking** are not trivia — they're modifiable leak and wound-healing risk factors worth flagging in IBD and cancer patients. Severe malnutrition may warrant preoperative optimization or delayed anastomosis.

## 6. The anastomotic leak — the single most important thing on this rotation

This is what kills people and what the whole team is quietly watching for. Recognize it early.

- **Incidence varies widely (~1–30%)** by location — **low rectal/coloanal anastomoses leak the most**, right-sided/ileocolic the least.
- **Classic timing: POD 5–7** (can be earlier, or present as late as 2–3 weeks; "late leaks" exist).
- **First sign is often tachycardia** and a clinical "something's off" *before* frank peritonitis. Then: fever, **leukocytosis, rising CRP/procalcitonin**, **failure to progress / new or persistent ileus**, increasing abdominal or pelvic pain, **purulent or feculent drain output**, and the telltale pattern of a patient who **was improving and then plateaus or backslides around POD 5.** (A **CRP that fails to fall by ~POD 3–4** is a useful early red flag.)
- Many signs are **neither sensitive nor specific**, and a diverting ileostomy can **mask** the presentation — which is exactly why you keep suspicion high.
- **Workup (hemodynamically stable):** **triple-contrast CT** (oral + rectal + IV) of abdomen/pelvis — rectal contrast is key for low anastomoses — plus labs and exam.
- **Risk factors:**
  - *Non-modifiable:* low (rectal) anastomosis / short distance from anal verge, **male sex / narrow pelvis**, emergency surgery, comorbidity, prior pelvic radiation.
  - *Modifiable:* **malnutrition/hypoalbuminemia, steroids/immunosuppression, smoking, obesity, perioperative blood transfusion**, possibly NSAIDs.
  - *Intraoperative:* **tension** (mitigated by splenic flexure mobilization), **poor perfusion** (assessed by ICG fluorescence angiography), contamination, technical factors. Higher-volume surgeons and stapled ileocolic anastomoses leak less.
- **Management** ranges from **antibiotics ± percutaneous drainage** (contained leak, stable patient) to **reoperation with washout and proximal diversion or takedown** (free leak, peritonitis, sepsis). **Delay in recognition directly increases mortality.**
- **The diverting loop ileostomy mitigates the *consequences* of a leak (less peritonitis/sepsis, fewer reoperations, lower mortality) — it does NOT prevent the leak itself.** This is a classic teaching point and a frequent pimp question.
- **The NSAID debate:** non-selective NSAIDs (especially ketorolac) have been associated in some studies with higher leak rates; practice varies. Know it's **debated, not settled.**

## 7. Benign and inflammatory disease — heavy at a referral center

### IBD surgery — UC vs Crohn's is a core distinction
| | Ulcerative colitis | Crohn's disease |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | Mucosal, **continuous**, rectum proximally, **colon only** | **Transmural, skip lesions, mouth-to-anus** |
| Surgery intent | **Curative** (remove colon + rectum) | **Palliative** — resect the problem, preserve length |
| Hallmark complications | Toxic megacolon, dysplasia/cancer, bleeding | **Fistulas, abscesses, strictures, perianal disease** |
| Definitive operation | Total proctocolectomy ± **IPAA** | Segmental resection / **strictureplasty** |

- **Acute severe UC (ASUC):** diagnosed by **Truelove and Witts criteria** (bloody stools + systemic signs). Treat with **IV corticosteroids**; if no response in ~3–5 days, **rescue infliximab or cyclosporine**; ongoing failure, perforation, refractory bleeding, or toxic megacolon → **surgery.**
- **Emergency UC surgery is a subtotal/total abdominal colectomy with end ileostomy** — **NOT** a pouch. Reasons: sick patients on high-dose steroids have far higher pouch complication rates, and the diagnosis may still be indeterminate. The rectum and pouch are dealt with later.
- **IPAA staging:**
  - **2-stage** (standard elective): (1) proctocolectomy + pouch + **diverting loop ileostomy**, (2) ileostomy reversal ~8–12 weeks later.
  - **3-stage** (sick / high-dose steroids / emergency / toxic megacolon): (1) total abdominal colectomy + end ileostomy, (2) completion proctectomy + pouch + diverting ileostomy, (3) ileostomy reversal. Safer staging when the patient is unoptimized.
- **Crohn's caution:** Crohn's is generally a **contraindication to IPAA** (high failure from fistulizing/recurrent disease), though selected Crohn's-colitis patients are offered pouches at expert centers. Many "pouch failures" labeled Crohn's are actually **mechanical** problems fixable with redo pouch surgery — a referral-center subspecialty.
- **Toxic megacolon:** colonic dilation (**>6 cm**) + systemic toxicity; complicates UC or C. diff. Surgical emergency if it doesn't reverse → **total abdominal colectomy with end ileostomy.**
- **Pouchitis:** the most common long-term IPAA complication; idiopathic inflammation of the pouch → treat with **antibiotics (ciprofloxacin/metronidazole).** Recurrent/refractory pouchitis raises suspicion for Crohn's of the pouch.
- **~10–15% of colitis is "indeterminate"** — can't be cleanly called UC vs Crohn's, which complicates the pouch decision.
- **Perioperative biologics and steroids (the timing question you'll hear discussed):** **high-dose corticosteroids** raise anastomotic/pouch complication and infection risk — a major reason emergency and steroid-dependent patients get **staged** operations with diversion rather than an immediate pouch. The data on **anti-TNF, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab** increasing post-op complications are mixed/reassuring, but their **timing relative to surgery** (and steroid taper) is a routine multidisciplinary discussion. Optimize **nutrition** and wean steroids when the clock allows.

### Diverticulitis — Hinchey classification and modern management
- **Modified Hinchey:**
  - **Ia** confined pericolic inflammation/phlegmon; **Ib** pericolic/mesocolic abscess.
  - **II** walled-off pelvic/retroperitoneal/distant abscess.
  - **III** purulent peritonitis.
  - **IV** feculent peritonitis (free perforation).
- **Diagnosis:** CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast is the test of choice (**sensitivity ~94%, specificity ~99%**).
- **Uncomplicated (Hinchey Ia):** increasingly **outpatient**; **oral antibiotics** shorten stays vs IV, and selected immunocompetent patients can be managed **without antibiotics** (DINAMO and related RCTs show equivalent outcomes). When antibiotics are used: **ciprofloxacin + metronidazole** or **amoxicillin-clavulanate** (outpatient). Sigmoid is the usual site (~60–70%); right-sided diverticulitis predominates in Asian populations.
- **Abscess (Hinchey Ib/II):** **percutaneous drainage** when ≥~4 cm + IV antibiotics (e.g., **piperacillin-tazobactam** or **ciprofloxacin/metronidazole**); smaller abscesses often resolve on antibiotics alone. **Failure to improve within ~48 h** of nonoperative management → escalate to surgery.
- **Perforated (Hinchey III/IV):** resection. **Hartmann** classically for unstable/feculent contamination, but **primary resection with anastomosis (± diverting loop ileostomy)** is increasingly preferred in **selected stable patients**, with lower overall morbidity and higher rates of eventual stoma reversal. **Laparoscopic lavage** is a bridging option in select Hinchey III.
- **Follow-up colonoscopy** (6–8 weeks) is recommended after **complicated** diverticulitis to exclude malignancy, but is **no longer routine after an uncomplicated first episode** (malignancy yield ~2–3%).
- **Elective sigmoidectomy** is individualized (recurrent episodes, complications, fistula like **colovesical** presenting with pneumaturia/fecaluria, stricture), not based on a fixed episode count.

### Ischemic colitis
- Lives at the **watershed zones** (splenic flexure/Griffiths, rectosigmoid/Sudeck). Older patient, low-flow or hypotensive event, **bloody diarrhea + crampy pain**, "**thumbprinting**" (submucosal edema) on imaging. **Rectum is usually spared** (dual blood supply). Mostly managed **medically** (bowel rest, IV fluids, antibiotics); surgery (segmental resection) for full-thickness necrosis, perforation, or peritonitis. Distinguish from **acute mesenteric ischemia** (SMA event, "pain out of proportion," small bowel, surgical emergency).

### C. difficile colitis (surgical angle)
- Fulminant C. diff (ileus, megacolon, shock, rising lactate, marked leukocytosis) failing maximal medical therapy → surgery: **total abdominal colectomy with end ileostomy**, or the colon-sparing **diverting loop ileostomy + intraoperative colonic lavage** with antegrade vancomycin.

### Anorectal disease (clinic and minor OR)
- **Cryptoglandular hypothesis:** obstruction/infection of anal glands at the dentate line is the common origin of **abscess → fistula.**
- **Anorectal abscess:** treat with **incision and drainage** (don't wait it out; antibiotics alone are insufficient). Antibiotics added for cellulitis, immunosuppression, diabetes, valvular disease. Named by space: perianal, ischiorectal, intersphincteric, supralevator.
- **Fistula-in-ano (Parks classification):** **intersphincteric, transsphincteric, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric.** **Goodsall's rule:** external opening **anterior** to the transverse anal line → **straight, radial** tract to a nearby internal opening; **posterior** (or anterior but >3 cm away) → **curved** tract to the **posterior midline.** Treatment depends on sphincter involvement: **fistulotomy** for low/simple tracts; **seton** (draining or cutting), **LIFT**, or advancement flap for complex/high tracts to protect continence. **Perianal Crohn's** fistulas: drain sepsis, place setons, then **medical therapy (anti-TNF) first.**
- **Anal fissure:** **posterior midline** tear (lateral or multiple fissures suggest Crohn's, HIV, TB, malignancy); severe pain with defecation + bright-red blood. Driven by **internal sphincter hypertonia/ischemia.** Medical first: fiber, sitz baths, **topical nitroglycerin or calcium-channel blocker (nifedipine/diltiazem)**, **botulinum toxin**; **lateral internal sphincterotomy** for refractory cases (small incontinence risk).
- **Hemorrhoids:** **internal graded I–IV** (I bleed/no prolapse, II prolapse-reduce spontaneously, III prolapse-need manual reduction, IV irreducible). **Rubber band ligation** for grades I–III; **hemorrhoidectomy** for grade III–IV or mixed disease (more effective, more painful); **stapled hemorrhoidopexy** an alternative for II–III. **Thrombosed external hemorrhoid:** excise if presenting **within ~72 h** (peak pain), otherwise treat supportively.
- **Pilonidal disease**, **rectal prolapse** (rectopexy ± resection; vs intussusception/internal prolapse), and **pelvic floor / defecatory dysfunction** also flow through this service.
- **Anal cancer** (squamous, HPV-related) is treated with **chemoradiation (Nigro protocol)**, not primary surgery — APR is salvage. Don't reflexively assume an anal lesion goes to the OR first.

## 8. Things you maybe didn't realize were relevant

- **Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS):** after sphincter-preserving rectal surgery, patients get **urgency, frequency, clustering, fragmentation, and incontinence.** It's a major quality-of-life burden and part of why organ-preserving strategies (watch-and-wait, radiation omission) are attractive. If a post-LAR patient complains about bowel habits, this has a name and a validated **LARS score.**
- **Prophylactic ureteral stents:** surgeons often have urology place stents before **redo pelvic, large tumor, or severe diverticular** cases because the **ureters get encased in inflammation.** Postop stents = that's why; watch urine output and for hematuria.
- **Presacral venous bleeding:** dreaded intraoperative complication during low rectal/TME dissection — the **presacral venous plexus** is valveless, high-volume, and very hard to control. You'll hear it referenced with appropriate dread.
- **Perineal wound after APR / pelvic exenteration:** large, slow-healing, sometimes **flap-reconstructed** (e.g., VRAM); a real, ongoing source of morbidity to track.
- **Why CEA, why MMR, why genetics:** these aren't checkbox labs. A young patient, right-sided or multiple tumors, and dMMR → **Lynch workup that changes the whole family's care.**
- **Hereditary syndromes you should recognize:**
  - **Lynch syndrome (HNPCC):** germline **MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, or EPCAM.** Identified now mainly by **universal tumor MMR/MSI testing** (the old **Amsterdam II** and **Bethesda** criteria miss ~half). Surveillance colonoscopy **every 1–2 years.** When CRC develops, many advocate **extended/subtotal colectomy** over segmental (high **metachronous** cancer risk). **Aspirin** reduces CRC risk (CAPP2). **Endometrial cancer is the #2 malignancy** — extracolonic surveillance matters.
  - **Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP):** germline **APC**, hundreds–thousands of adenomas, near-100% cancer risk → **prophylactic colectomy/proctocolectomy** (often TAC-IRA or proctocolectomy-IPAA) typically in late teens/20s. Plus **upper GI/duodenal surveillance** (ampullary/duodenal cancer) and **desmoid tumor** risk (intra-abdominal, can preclude pouch).
  - **MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP):** **autosomal recessive (biallelic MUTYH)**, attenuated-polyposis phenotype — worth knowing it's the recessive mimic of FAP.
- **Synchronous (3–5%) and metachronous cancers:** the reason for full preoperative colonoscopy and lifelong surveillance.
- **Drains tell stories:** know each drain's **volume and character** every day. **Feculent/purulent or a sudden change around POD 5** = think leak.
- **Why the loop-ileostomy patient bounces back in a month:** **dehydration from high output → AKI → readmission**, not the incision. Counsel hydration + loperamide before discharge.
- **ICG fluorescence angiography:** surgeons inject indocyanine green to **assess anastomotic perfusion** intraoperatively — perfusion is a leak risk you can sometimes see in the OR.

## 9. How to be useful (and earn a good eval without loving the OR)

- **Prerounds checklist per patient:** POD#, operation + indication, vitals and **trends (especially HR)**, **I&Os**, **stoma and drain outputs (volume + character)**, flatus/stool, diet status, lines/tubes/Foley, pain regimen (and opioid-sparing progress), ambulation, key labs (**WBC, lytes, Cr, Mg, Hgb, lactate** if sick), antibiotics + day, VTE prophylaxis.
- **Present in surgical cadence — tight and forward-looking:** "POD3 from sigmoidectomy for a diverticular stricture, afebrile, HR 70s–80s, tolerating regular diet, ostomy 600 mL over 24h, passing flatus, ambulating, pain controlled on PO. Plan: Foley out, transition fully to PO analgesia, continue ERAS, anticipate discharge POD4–5."
- **In the OR:** know **today's** relevant anatomy (TME plane, ureter, vascular pedicle), ask **one** good thinking question, otherwise retract well and watch the planes. You don't have to perform enthusiasm.
- **The data the team cares about most:** trending **heart rate**, **stoma/drain output**, **return of bowel function**, and whether the patient is **progressing or backsliding.**
- **Be the student who flags the POD5 tachycardia.** Anticipating the leak conversation is worth a month of forced enthusiasm.

## 10. Rapid-fire pimp answers

- Colon watershed areas → **splenic flexure (Griffiths)**, **rectosigmoid (Sudeck).**
- SMA–IMA collaterals → **marginal artery of Drummond**, **arc of Riolan.**
- Most common CRC met site → **liver** (portal drainage); **low rectal/anal can go to lung directly** (systemic drainage).
- Most common cause of large bowel obstruction in adults → **colorectal cancer (~60%)**, then **volvulus**, then **diverticular stricture.**
- Minimum nodes for staging → **≥12.**
- Dentate line significance → transition of **epithelium, innervation, blood supply, lymphatic drainage, and cancer type**; explains internal vs external hemorrhoid behavior.
- IMA ligation risk → distal colonic ischemia if the **marginal artery** is poor (mitigated by splenic flexure mobilization).
- Structure at risk during splenic flexure mobilization / IMA ligation → the **left ureter** (crosses the iliac vessels at the pelvic brim; identify and protect it).
- Strongest predictor of local recurrence in rectal cancer → **quality of TME + negative CRM.**
- Diverting ileostomy after LAR → **mitigates the consequences of a leak, does not prevent it.**
- Anastomotic leak timing/first sign → **POD 5–7; tachycardia and failure to progress** before peritonitis.
- Imaging for suspected leak → **triple-contrast (oral/rectal/IV) CT.**
- UC vs Crohn's surgery → **UC curative** (proctocolectomy/IPAA); **Crohn's segmental/length-preserving**, IPAA generally contraindicated.
- Emergency UC operation → **total abdominal colectomy with end ileostomy** (not a pouch).
- Toxic megacolon → colon **>6 cm** + toxicity → emergent **subtotal colectomy + end ileostomy.**
- Hinchey III/IV → resection; **Hartmann** vs **primary anastomosis ± diversion** (the latter increasingly favored in stable patients).
- Abscess drainage threshold in diverticulitis → **percutaneous if ≥~4 cm.**
- Anal fissure location → **posterior midline** (atypical/lateral → think Crohn's, HIV, malignancy).
- Goodsall's rule → **anterior = straight radial**, **posterior = curved to posterior midline.**
- Hemorrhoid banding vs surgery → **band grades I–III**, **hemorrhoidectomy III–IV**; thrombosed external excise **<72 h.**
- Anal (squamous) cancer treatment → **chemoradiation (Nigro)**, surgery is salvage.
- #1 cause of readmission after colorectal surgery → **dehydration from high ostomy output.**
- Why MMR/MSI on every tumor → **Lynch screening + immunotherapy eligibility** (dMMR rectal → dostarlimab, possible organ preservation); MLH1 loss → check **BRAF/methylation** to exclude sporadic.
- Anti-EGFR works only in → **RAS wild-type** tumors **and** left-sided primaries (right-sided respond poorly even if RAS-WT).
- First-line for **BRAF V600E** metastatic CRC → **encorafenib + cetuximab + chemo** (BREAKWATER); ~10%, poor prognosis.
- dMMR/MSI-high CRC + immunotherapy → metastatic **nivo+ipi** first-line; stage III colon **adjuvant atezolizumab+FOLFOX** (ATOMIC); neoadjuvant **nivo+ipi** in colon (NICHE) and **dostarlimab** in rectal — immuno is moving earlier.
- Average-risk CRC screening start age → **45** (early-onset CRC rising); blood-based **Shield** test now an option, colonoscopy still gold standard.
- Stage III colon adjuvant → **FOLFOX or CAPOX**, 3 vs 6 months risk-adapted (**IDEA**); oxaliplatin → **cumulative neuropathy.**
- PROSPECT takeaway → intermediate-risk rectal cancer can often get **FOLFOX with selective radiation omission.**
- Nerve causing retrograde ejaculation → **sympathetic (superior hypogastric plexus).**
- Nerves for erection/bladder → **parasympathetic pelvic splanchnics (S2–S4).**

## 11. Daily routine, survival logistics, and discharge criteria

### The day
- **Arrive early (~5:00–5:30 AM)** to preround. Surgery runs ahead of the clock; being late once is remembered.
- **Preround data to gather per patient:** overnight events, vitals + trends (especially **HR**), **I&Os**, **drain outputs (volume + character)**, **stoma output (color, volume)**, **labs (CBC, BMP, ± lactate)**, wound/incision check, and ask the patient directly about **pain, flatus/BM, nausea, ambulation, and diet tolerance.** Have your note or pre-round card ready before rounds.
- **Rounding presentation (concise, surgical cadence):** one-liner → overnight events → vitals/I&Os → focused exam → labs → **assessment + plan toward discharge.** For post-ops, lead with **POD# + procedure** and frame everything around discharge criteria.

### Discharge criteria (ERAS targets)
- **Tolerating diet, passing flatus/BM, pain controlled on PO meds, ambulating independently, afebrile, and (if present) stoma functioning with acceptable output.** Don't discharge a fresh ileostomy until output is controlled — high output is the top readmission driver.

### What to carry
- **Trauma shears, penlight, stethoscope, a pocket reference, and a couple of pens.** Knowing where to find **op notes, pathology, and imaging** quickly in Epic is its own skill — set up your patient lists and reports ahead of time.

### How to be genuinely useful
- Offer to help with **dressing changes, drain management, and stoma care.** See and present **consults** proactively. Read about **tomorrow's cases tonight.** Know your patients cold — labs, imaging, pathology. When unsure, **go examine the patient.**

## 12. OR etiquette and technical basics

### Before the case
- **Review the case the night before:** the patient's history, imaging, pathology, and the **planned procedure + relevant anatomy** (the plane, the vessels, the ureter for that operation).
- Know whether it's **open, lap, or robotic**, and what your likely role is.

### In the room
- **Introduce yourself to the scrub tech and circulating nurse** — they run the room and will help you if you're respectful. Ask **where you can stand** and **what you can touch.**
- **Scrub in early.** Keep your hands above your waist and in front of you; assume everything below the waist and behind you is non-sterile.
- **Assisting:** hold retractors **steadily** (constant, quiet tension — don't drift), **follow the camera** smoothly if you're driving the laparoscope, and **suction where the surgeon is looking, not where you want to.**
- **Cutting sutures:** cut to a **~3 mm tail unless told otherwise** (shorter for fine/deep sutures, longer when the knot will be retied or for a planned tie-back). Cut with the tips, flat to the suture, and pause for the "cut" cue rather than guessing.
- **Know your knots:** be able to **two-hand tie** and **instrument tie** competently; practice on any spare suture, in the workroom, anywhere. Volunteering "can I throw a few?" at the right moment is how you earn reps.
- **Anticipate, don't narrate.** Watch the field and have the next move ready; ask **one** good question per case rather than a running commentary.

## 14. Red flags — when to grab a resident (quick card)

Don't sit on these. State the finding, the trend, and your concern.

- **POD 5-ish tachycardia + failure to progress** (new ileus, plateau, climbing WBC/CRP) → possible **anastomotic leak.**
- **Dusky, black, or retracted stoma** → **stomal ischemia.**
- **Stoma output stops + distension + nausea/vomiting** → **obstruction or outlet obstruction.**
- **High ostomy output + falling urine output / rising Cr** → **dehydration/AKI** heading toward readmission.
- **Pain out of proportion to exam** → think **mesenteric ischemia.**
- **Diffuse peritonitis** (rigid, rebound, guarding), hemodynamic instability, rising lactate → surgical emergency.
- **Sudden change in drain character** (new purulent/feculent/high-volume) → leak/abscess.
- **Brisk lower GI bleeding** with instability → escalate, don't observe.
- **Fever + leukocytosis after initially improving** → look for a source (leak, organ-space SSI, line, C. diff).
